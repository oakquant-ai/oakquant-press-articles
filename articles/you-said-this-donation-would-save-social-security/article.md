A hypothetical American experiment in making campaign promises partially self-funding.

The email arrived at 7:43 a.m.

URGENT, it said. Social Security was in danger. The country was at a crossroads. Only Martin's immediate contribution could prevent catastrophe.

Martin was 72. He had received approximately the same warning from six organizations since breakfast.

He clicked the donation link.

Then he noticed something new.

> Your contribution: $200
>
> Your remaining small-donor allowance: $100
>
> To your selected political recipient: $150
>
> To Social Security: $50

Martin read it twice.

For the first time in his life, an email asking him to save Social Security had included a mechanism for doing so.

## The American Political Checkout

In this hypothetical America, Congress had established a central clearinghouse for covered electoral contributions.

Nobody agreed on its official name. Its working title was the American Political Checkout, which narrowly defeated the Federal Bureau of Putting Your Money Where Your Mouth Is.

The rules were straightforward:

- Each eligible contributor received a $100 exemption per election cycle, adjusted for inflation.
- The allowance applied across covered recipients, not separately to every donation.
- Above the allowance, half went to the contributor's chosen political recipient and half went to Social Security.
- Covered committees registered and used the same system.
- Transfers retained their funding history, so moving money did not create another exemption or trigger another levy.
- Regulators could audit the full trail; public disclosure followed defined rules with personal-information safeguards.

The clearinghouse did not choose candidates for donors. It simply introduced an unfamiliar participant into political fundraising: an existing public obligation.

## Denise Gives $25 and Gets On With Her Life

Denise was a nurse who wanted to give $25 to a congressional challenger.

She contributed, received confirmation that her entire $25 had reached the campaign, and went to work.

Her remaining exemption was $75.

No tax return supplement. No televised panel about her motives. No requirement to explain whether her political philosophy was consistent with something she had posted in 2014.

Later, she gave $50 to another covered committee. That contribution also passed through untouched.

Denise had contributed $75 to political causes. The system had protected her modest participation.

This disappointed several commentators who had already booked a segment titled "Is the Government Coming for Your $25?"

## Martin Discovers That His Donation Has Two Constituencies

Martin's $200 contribution was his first of the cycle.

The first $100 went entirely to his selected recipient. The remaining $100 split evenly.

The campaign received $150. Social Security received $50.

Martin did not receive a larger benefit check because of this. The payment supported the program; it did not purchase additional benefit rights.

He also did not receive a commemorative tote bag from Social Security, although he felt this was an oversight.

At lunch, his friend asked whether the scheme would save the program.

"No," Martin said. "But apparently neither did the last fourteen emails."

## Grant Discovers That Division Is Not a Loophole

Grant was wealthy, politically engaged, and confident that most rules were invitations to schedule a meeting.

He wanted to contribute $1 million to a super PAC. Under long-standing federal campaign-finance rules, the committee was properly registered and legally permitted to accept unlimited sums. It was simply unaccustomed to sharing the routing number with the Treasury.

With his full $100 allowance available, the checkout allocated:

- $500,050 to the super PAC.
- $499,950 to Social Security.

Grant called his adviser.

"What if we make ten thousand donations of $100?"

"The allowance follows you," she said.

"What if we use different recipients?"

"Still you."

Grant paused.

He had encountered a database with an unusually firm understanding of personal identity.

## Citizens for a Brighter Tomorrow Meets Its Funding History

Across town, a political organization called Citizens for a Brighter Tomorrow submitted its registration.

Its funding source was listed as Americans for a Better Yesterday.

That organization had received its money from Tomorrow Holdings.

Tomorrow Holdings preferred not to discuss yesterday.

In the real world, registration alone rarely resolves this: an organization can be entirely visible on paper while the original sources behind its money remain obscured. Here, however, the clearinghouse requested the upstream funding trail.

The organization's attorney expressed concern that excessive attribution could discourage civic participation.

The compliance officer expressed concern that the paperwork contained three uplifting organizational names and no explanation of where the money had actually originated.

Under the clearinghouse rules, covered funds carried their history through transfers. A dollar did not become a fresh dollar merely because it had spent the afternoon inside a holding company.

## A Foreign State Actor Requests the Premium Package

An operative representing a foreign government asked whether there was a higher rate for overseas customers.

Perhaps 75%.

Perhaps 90%.

His government was prepared to discuss a substantial investment in American retirement security, provided it could first make a modest investment in American electoral outcomes.

The answer was no.

Federal law already prohibits foreign-national election contributions and expenditures, while protecting the rights of U.S. citizens living abroad and lawful permanent residents. The clearinghouse left that boundary intact rather than establishing a duty-free diplomatic tier.

The operative asked whether the payment could be described as a bilateral competitiveness partnership.

The answer remained no.

There was no premium package.

## The Social Security Coffers Receive a Deposit

At the Office of the Chief Actuary, two analysts stared at a terminal notification.

"Political fundraising?" one asked.

"Yes."

"Are they borrowing our name for an email subject line again?"

"Apparently they brought money this time."

The excitement was understandable. The scale required perspective.

Social Security took in approximately $1.449 trillion in 2025 and incurred approximately $1.609 trillion in costs, drawing down reserves by $160 billion.

The actuaries accepted the deposit.

They did not cancel the afternoon solvency meeting.

## What If We Had Started Ten Years Earlier?

At this point, the satire must briefly surrender the microphone to a spreadsheet.

Across the five completed federal election cycles covering 2015-2024, OpenSecrets reports approximately $51.12 billion in nominal election spending.

Spending is not the same as contributions subject to this proposal. But as a rough scale exercise, suppose 75% represented amounts subject to the split after exemptions. Half of that would have generated approximately $19.2 billion for Social Security.

That assumes no change in donor behavior and no administrative friction. At 50% coverage, receipts would have been about $12.8 billion; at 100%, about $25.6 billion.

Under the middle assumption, crediting receipts at each cycle's end and applying an illustrative 3% annual return produces a balance of roughly $21.8 billion by the end of 2025.

Useful money. Not a rescue.

The $19.2 billion in receipts would equal about 12% of the reserve drawdown in 2025 alone.

The spreadsheet returned the microphone.

## What If We Start With This Election?

A 2026 launch would apply prospectively. Money already raised and spent would not reappear because Congress had installed a better checkout page.

Using the average annualized spending of the 2022 and 2024 cycles, the same assumptions produce roughly $2.23 billion in annual receipts. Over ten years, that means approximately:

- $22.3 billion with no growth in the underlying base.
- $28.0 billion with 5% annual nominal growth.
- $35.5 billion with 10% annual nominal growth.

Those are illustrations, not forecasts. They exclude investment returns, administrative costs, and the possibility that donors might respond to losing half their political purchasing power.

Nor do they establish a particular number of additional solvent years. Current trustees' projections put retirement-and-survivors reserve depletion in 2032 and hypothetical combined-fund depletion in 2034. Continuing tax income would still pay a portion of scheduled benefits.

A new bucket helps. It does not become a drainage system because the bucket has bipartisan branding.

## The Constitutional Lawyer Arrives

Just as everyone began enjoying the proposal, a constitutional lawyer entered.

She raised questions about compelled disclosure, associational privacy, and diverting half of covered contributions above the exemption.

These were serious questions, not evidence that she secretly opposed retirement.

Contributions and expenditures receive different First Amendment treatment. Disclosure rules face exacting scrutiny. A cleaner API would not make the Supreme Court disappear.

The author removed "Obviously Constitutional" from the slide deck.

The lawyer also requested a contingency plan for payment-gateway outages.

This was accepted after the conference room projector briefly stopped working.

## A Modest Improvement in the Meaning of Help

The proposal would not save Social Security by itself. That would still require Congress to do something considerably more difficult than approve a checkout page.

But it would introduce a small correspondence between the promise in a fundraising email and the destination of the money.

The split would apply whether a solicitation mentioned Social Security or not. But when one did, some of the money would finally go where the subject line suggested.

Martin's next fundraising email arrived at 6:12 p.m.

"Only you can help protect Social Security," it said.

He looked at the checkout.

For once, "help" had a line item.

This is satire and a hypothetical policy thought experiment. The proposed system does not exist; all characters, organizations, dialogue, and administrative scenes are fictional.
