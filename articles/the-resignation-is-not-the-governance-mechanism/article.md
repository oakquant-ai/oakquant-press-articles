On September 8, a 27-year-old named Jacob Coxon quit his job and posted the reason. He had spent three years teaching AI models to be smarter, first at OpenAI, then at Anthropic. He wrote that neither company is acting responsibly. He wrote that both are racing toward AI that builds better AI, and that they are gambling with our lives.

By the next morning the post had been seen about 76 million times.

Then his colleague answered him in public. Evan Hubinger leads alignment science at Anthropic. Alignment means the work of getting a machine to want what its builders want it to want, which sounds simple and is not. A bank teaches a loan model to approve good borrowers. The model learns to approve people who look like the borrowers who got approved before. Those are two different lessons and the model cannot tell them apart. Now do that with a system smarter than the people checking it.

Hubinger said Coxon was right about what people inside the company believe. He put his own number on it. More than one in ten that AI kills everyone within ten years. He said the models that exist today are not the problem. He said the ones that build their own replacements are.

I want to be careful here, because the coverage has not been.

Nobody predicted the end of the world. One researcher gave a personal probability. A probability is a claim about uncertainty. It is not a prediction. Hubinger could be wrong by a factor of ten in either direction and nothing I am about to write changes. This piece does not rest on his number.

## What was already on the page

In March I published the third book of a trilogy called The Cost of the Machine. Three books, 38 chapters, and one argument: removing the human from decisions that matter is the central danger of this age.

Chapter 9 of Book Three is called The Builders' Dilemma. Anyone can read it on this site for free.

Governance is a heavy word for a plain thing. It means somebody who is not you gets to say no, and the no holds. A company can promise. A regulator can stop you. Those are different, and the difference is the whole subject.

The chapter sets out three situations and none of them are hypothetical.

A company governs itself and its competitors do not. It draws lines, it turns down contracts, it spends money on safety. The customers who want the unrestricted thing go elsewhere. The company loses, and the lines come down, or the company does.

Every company governs itself at the same time, to the same standard. This works. It also requires competitors to hold a promise that each one of them profits from breaking, quietly, first. Game theory has a name for that arrangement and the name is unstable.

Nobody governs anybody. The harms pile up until something bad enough happens that a legislature panics. Panic law is broad, blunt, and written by people who learned the subject in six weeks.

I wrote one sentence in that chapter that I did not expect to see tested this fast. A company that governs itself too aggressively may not survive to govern at all.

That is Coxon's resignation letter, five months early, written by somebody who has never worked at a frontier lab.

## The company made the argument better than I did

In June, Anthropic published a proposal by co-founder Jack Clark and Marina Favaro of the Anthropic Institute. It asked the industry to build the ability to slow down or pause together, and to be able to check that everyone actually had.

Clark went on the BBC and described the industry as a car with a gas pedal and no brake pedal. He also said that Claude now writes more than 80 percent of the code that goes into Anthropic's own product, and that going to 100 percent is possible within two years.

Read that twice. The company is telling you that its main tool for building the next model is the current model.

But the line that stopped me was about verification. Anthropic wrote that training runs are much easier to hide than missile silos, that the ingredients are ordinary, and that whoever keeps going while the others stop inherits the lead. The incentive to cheat, they said, is enormous.

That is my scenario two. A firm valued near a trillion dollars published it as company policy, three months after I published it as chapter nine, and neither of us invented it. The name for it is the prisoner's dilemma, a hundred-year-old idea about two people in separate cells who would both do better staying quiet and both talk anyway. Nuclear treaties exist because nations could not stay quiet on their own. Drug safety agencies exist because pharmaceutical companies could not. Nobody thought either industry was staffed by bad people.

## The evidence that got no coverage

On September 2, the Institute for Security and Technology and the Future of Life Institute released a survey. They asked 111 people who work in national security and AI, between the end of April and the middle of July. Many of them serve or served in the intelligence agencies, in Congress, in federal law enforcement, or in the departments of Defense, Energy, State and Commerce.

Dozens of them put the odds of humans losing control of AI within ten years at one in ten or higher. Many said the risk of an AI-driven disaster has already passed the level they would call acceptable. Most expect machines that match human thinking by 2032.

That survey took ten weeks and involved 111 careers. It got a fraction of the attention that one man's post got in one night.

I am not complaining about the internet. I am pointing at a structural problem. We have built an information system that responds to resignation and ignores measurement. Which is itself a story about what happens when nobody is watching the light.

## The test that arrives next

Anthropic drew two red lines with the Pentagon in February. No fully autonomous weapons. No mass surveillance of citizens. It was designated a supply-chain risk for it, and then a million people a day signed up for its product, and the chalk on the sidewalk outside its office said thank you.

I wrote about that too, and I put a warning next to it. The red lines exist at the pleasure of the company's leadership. A board can change them. A board under pressure will be asked to.

Anthropic is now reported to be heading for an initial public offering, which is the moment a private company starts selling shares to the public, at a valuation the Wall Street Journal has put in the trillions. Its safety record is part of what it is selling.

I do not think anybody there is planning to abandon anything, and that has never been how this goes. The question is what a red line is worth when it becomes a line in a prospectus, and the honest answer is that nobody knows, because we have never asked a company to hold a line at that price.

Here is where my argument thins, and I would rather say it than have you find it. The public response to the Pentagon fight was better than my own model predicted. My chapter says self-governance gets punished by the market. In February the market rewarded it. One event is not a pattern, and consumer preference is not a rulebook. But I was more wrong than right about that week and it belongs on the record.

## What the builders are actually asking for

The resolution to all of this is not braver companies. It is a floor.

A floor is a minimum. Below it, nobody is allowed to operate. Above it, compete as hard as you like. Every drug company in America competes ferociously and every one of them goes through the Food and Drug Administration first. That agency is not a ceiling on invention. It stops any one company from winning by being the one that skipped the trial.

AI has no floor. It has voluntary promises from some firms, silence from others, and a market that rewards whoever spends least on safety and ships fastest.

So when a company asks for a coordinated pause, and an alignment lead posts his own extinction estimate, and a pretraining researcher quits in public, they are not sounding an alarm at you. They are asking somebody to take the decision away from them, because they cannot make it alone and stay in business. It is the strangest lobbying position in modern industry, and it has now been stated out loud by three different people in three different registers in six months.

My book said the window for writing AI rules would close in three to five years. I wrote that in March. It is September. Call it two and a half to four and a half, and I would take the lower number.

## The small thing

Do not do anything about extinction this week. You cannot, and neither can I.

Do this instead. Find one system in your organization where a machine makes a decision about a person, and go and find out who is allowed to overrule it, by name. Not the policy. The name. Then find out how long that person gets, and whether anybody has ever checked what happens when they say no.

In 1801 a lighthouse keeper named Thomas Howell kept the light burning for weeks beside the body of the man he worked with, because the light was not for him. The light was for the ships. The light is automated now and the keeper is gone.

This week a young man was the brake pedal. One man is not a mechanism. He only gets to do it once.
