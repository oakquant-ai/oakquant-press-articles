The coach stood in front of a bank of microphones in Oxford, Mississippi. It was the fall of 1998. A reporter asked if he might leave Ole Miss for a bigger job.

He smiled. They would have to carry him out, he said, in a pine box.

Say you are one of his players. You are nineteen and you play linebacker. You picked this school partly because of this man. He came to your house and he ate your mother's pound cake and he told her he would look after you. You hear the pine-box line on the evening news and you believe it. Why wouldn't you.

A few days later he took the head job at Auburn.

His name was Tommy Tuberville. Six years after the pine box, his Auburn team went thirteen and zero.

Now go and find an Auburn fan and ask about the pine box. Watch the face. There is a small shrug, and a smile, and then a sentence about how coaching is a business. Ask an Ole Miss fan the same question and you will get a longer answer. It may not be suitable for children.

Both fans heard the same promise. Both know how it ended. One of them got the coach who left and the other got the coach who won, and the promise weighs a different amount depending on which seat you had.

Robert Louis Stevenson wrote a whole book about a man with two faces, the good Dr. Jekyll and the brutal Mr. Hyde. Stevenson needed a potion to change Jekyll into Hyde. Public life often needs only a different seat in the stands. The coach never changed at all. The face you saw depended on where you were sitting, and that seat is what this story is about. We say loyalty matters. We say it and we mean it. We also want the coach who can win. Most of the time we never have to choose which belief comes first, because nobody forces us to. Then somebody does.

## We forgive almost anything that finally moves

Here is a test. You work at a company that has been about to launch the same product for three years. There have been six planning retreats. There have been forty-one slide decks, and one of them had a slide that just said "Alignment." Every quarter the launch date moves back one quarter, like a hiker who keeps seeing the summit behind the next hill.

Then a new boss arrives. On her first day she cancels every standing meeting. By the end of her first week she has killed two side projects that three vice presidents loved. By the end of her first month she ships the thing. How do you feel the next morning?

If you are honest, you feel relief. It sits low in your stomach like a warm drink. You might also notice that she was rude to two people you like, and that one of them cried in the parking lot. You notice it. Then the product ships, and the noticing gets quieter. That quieting is the part worth watching.

In 1997 Steve Jobs came back to Apple. The company had been losing money for years and it sold a pile of computers that even its own staff had trouble telling apart. Jobs drew a grid on a whiteboard. Two columns, for home users and for professionals. Two rows, for desktops and for laptops. Four boxes. Anything that did not fit in a box was cut. Most of the product line went in a single year.

You know how that story ends. Apple became one of the most valuable companies on Earth, and the whiteboard became a legend.

But run the same story with a different ending. Say the four boxes had failed and Apple had gone broke in 1999. Nobody would call the grid visionary. They would call it reckless, and they would tell stories about the good people Jobs pushed out. The man at the whiteboard would be exactly the same man. Only the ending would have changed, and with it, which face we saw.

Results reach back in time and edit our opinion of how they were made. A win makes the rough method look brave. A loss makes the same method look careless. We hand the winner a kind of credit card, and we let them charge the next decision to it before we see the bill.

Not every rule protects the public. Some meetings exist so that nobody has to own a hard choice. The new boss who cancels them may be doing everyone a favor. The trouble is that relief does not come with a receipt. It feels exactly the same whether the method was sound or whether we just got lucky.

## The same decision looks different from Oxford and from Baton Rouge

College football is useful here because it lets us watch ourselves keep score in real time.

Try this one. You are a tight end at Ole Miss in November 2025. Your team is good, better than it has been in years. You are headed to the playoff. Then your head coach, Lane Kiffin, takes the job at Louisiana State. He says he asked to stay and coach you through the playoff, and the athletic director said no. The athletic director says Kiffin knew weeks ago that staying was never an option. You play the biggest games of your life for somebody else. Would you ever play for him again?

In real life, two of Kiffin's Ole Miss starters, a tight end named Dae'Quan Wright and a defensive lineman named Zxavian Harris, finished college and went to professional training camps in the summer of 2026. They had short stints on pro rosters. Then they left those camps and enrolled at Louisiana State, to play one more college season for the coach who had left them.

People are not simple. That is the first lesson.

The second lesson came from the Southeastern Conference, the league that both schools belong to. The league did not want former pro players back in college football. In August the conference unanimously approved a rule barring former pro players. In a separate vote on penalties, fifteen schools voted yes and Louisiana State abstained. The penalties were not small. A coach who broke the rule could be suspended for half a season. The school could be fined half of its whole football budget.

The players went to court in Baton Rouge. A judge ordered that the league could not punish a school for playing them. The conference had already sued Louisiana State, and Kiffin by name, in federal court in Alabama. When the season opener against Clemson arrived, the two players were not on the roster. So who was right?

Inside the Louisiana State locker room, Kiffin looked like a coach doing exactly what coaches are hired to do. He found a door that others had not tried, and he walked two good players through it. From the conference office the same move looked like one team rewriting the rules of a competition that belongs to sixteen teams. A judge can make it legal for a player to play. A judge cannot make everyone agree that the rule was a bad one.

Both sides have a point. That sentence is easy to write and hard to live with. Most of us, if we are honest, decide which side has the better point by checking which jersey we are wearing.

In football, at least, the damage has limits. A fan who is fed up can buy a ticket somewhere else. A player can transfer. Everybody gets another season. Now take the sidelines away.

## Pressure is a tool, and somebody else usually pays for it

Tuberville did not leave coaching behind when he went into politics. His Senate website still calls him Coach. Alabama elected him to the Senate in 2020.

Try one more. This officer is invented, but hundreds of real ones lived some version of her year. You are a Navy officer. You have spent nineteen years earning a promotion and every person who needs to sign off on it has signed. You have told your kids they are moving. Your spouse has turned down a job in the old city. The new job starts in six weeks.

Then one senator puts a hold on it. A Senate hold lets one senator slow nominations that otherwise have broad support. The hold has nothing to do with you. It is about a Defense Department policy you had no part in writing. You wait. The six weeks pass. So do six more.

In 2023 Tuberville did this to more than four hundred senior military officers at once. He was protesting a Pentagon policy that paid travel costs for service members who had to leave their state to get reproductive care, including abortion. For about ten months the promotions sat still. Some officers did two jobs at once. Congress's own watchdog later found no demonstrated effect on unit readiness, but it documented disruption for some families. Some could not move to their new posts, enroll their children in school on time, or start new jobs. In December he dropped most of the holds. The policy he was fighting stayed in place until a new administration dropped it in 2025. The holds did not end it. The next administration did.

People who agreed with him saw a senator using one of the few tools a single senator has. Without the holds, they said, the Pentagon would never have listened. People who disagreed saw service members used as hostages in a fight they could not join. Both groups looked at the same stalled promotions and came to opposite conclusions.

That episode asks a harder question than whether you liked the tactic. It asks what we mean when we call something effective.

The holds certainly created pressure. They got attention. They slowed down a whole branch of government. But pressure and results are two different things. You can push on a door with all your weight for ten months and the door can still be locked. And neither the pressure nor the result tells you whether the officer and her kids should have been the ones holding the bill.

Politics runs on moves like this. A strike, a veto, a boycott, a hold, a threat to cut off funding. Each one creates a cost in one place to force a choice in another. Supporters call it hardball. Opponents call it coercion. Very often both words are partly true.

Here is the uncomfortable part. We tend to examine the tactic closely only when we dislike the cause. When we like the cause, the tactic starts to look like courage.

## The man who made the call on television

For fourteen seasons, millions of Americans watched Donald Trump sit at the head of a boardroom table and decide things. The Apprentice gave every episode a clean frame. There was a task and two teams and a fight in the boardroom. Then one man pointed at somebody and said, "You're fired."

It was television, and everyone knew it was television. But years of watching one person make the final call leaves a mark. Researchers who studied the show's audience later found that people who watched more of it were more likely to back Trump in the 2016 Republican primary. That does not explain a whole political movement. A show cannot do that. It would still be strange to pretend all those evenings in front of the boardroom had no effect at all.

Government has none of television's tools. There is no editor to cut away from the people who have to live with a decision. There is no final episode that proves the boss was right. The wish for someone who will finally make the call survives the move from the studio to the capital anyway.

His critics often miss what his supporters feel. It is the relief of watching somebody press hard on a problem that other leaders only talked about.

Take defense spending in Europe. For years NATO, which binds its members to defend each other, had a guideline that each of them spend two percent of their national income on defense. It was a guideline, not a binding rule, and many of them missed it year after year. Trump complained about this for a decade, loudly and not always politely.

In June 2025 the members agreed to a new target for 2035. They would spend 3.5 percent on core defense and another 1.5 percent on wider security, like protecting power grids and ports. Two cents of every dollar became five.

A supporter can look at that and say, see, this is what I meant. People called his methods improper, and now an old imbalance is getting fixed.

It is a fair point, and it is not the whole story. Russia's war in Ukraine frightened Europe far more than any American speech could. Treating the new target as the work of one man is bad history. Pretending his pressure played no part is bad history too.

Someone else can look at the same story and ask a different question. How much trust did it cost? Will countries actually hit the target, or will 2035 arrive the way the old two percent deadlines did? Reuters has reported both the rise in European spending and the new doubts in Europe about whether America will stay. Something moved. That is true. What kind of alliance is left after it moved is a separate question, and it is still open.

The same pressure travels. In July 2025 Trump announced a fifty percent tariff on many goods from Brazil, though some, like orange juice and aircraft parts, were spared. A tariff that size can cost a Brazilian exporter an American buyer in a single season. Trump tied the tariff in part to a court case in Brazil against his friend and ally, the former president Jair Bolsonaro. He called the case a witch hunt.

So here is the question that matters more than any verdict on Trump. If you believe an institution has failed for years, how hard do you let someone push on your behalf? At what point do you stop calling it necessary and start asking who has to live with it?

That question should make you a little uncomfortable. It should stay uncomfortable when the leader is one you voted for.

## The judge looks fair when the verdict goes your way

Go south for a minute and pull up a chair.

Say you live in São Paulo. Your family is invented, but millions of Brazilian families would recognize it. For twenty years your family has eaten barbecue at your uncle's house most weekends. Somewhere around 2018 the lunch started to come in two colors. Half the family wears the yellow-and-green shirt of the national football team, which became the uniform of Bolsonaro's supporters. The other half wears red, the color of Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva and his Workers' Party. The meat is still good. Nobody talks about judges anymore.

They used to. In 2017 a federal judge named Sergio Moro convicted Lula of corruption. In April 2018 Lula went to prison. He stayed there for 580 days, and he was barred from that year's election. Bolsonaro won it. Then Bolsonaro made Moro his justice minister.

If you wore yellow, the judge was a hero. He had finally jailed a powerful man everyone said was untouchable. If you wore red, the judge had just taken a job from the man his verdict helped elect.

In 2021 Brazil's Supreme Court threw out Lula's convictions. It said the case had been heard in the wrong court, and it later ruled that Moro had not been impartial. The deciding vote on Moro came from Justice Cármen Lúcia. Lula ran again in 2022 and beat Bolsonaro by 50.9 percent to 49.1. A week after the new president took office, thousands of Bolsonaro supporters stormed the Congress, the Supreme Court and the presidential palace in Brasília.

In September 2025 a panel of the same Supreme Court convicted Bolsonaro of plotting a coup to stay in power after that election. Four of the five justices found him guilty, and Cármen Lúcia was one of them. He was sentenced to twenty-seven years and three months. This spring Congress overrode Lula's veto of a law that could shorten the sentence, and whether it applies to him is still being fought over. Brazil votes on October 4, and recent polls put Lula and Bolsonaro's son Flávio in a near tie in a potential runoff.

Now count the table. One family. Two colors. Two presidents, both convicted by Brazilian courts, and one of them cleared and elected again. Each half of the family has called the courts heroes, and each half has called them crooks, and each switched at almost the exact moment the verdict switched sides.

I am not saying the two cases are the same. A corruption case thrown out over a biased judge and a coup plot proved to four justices are very different things. The habit behind the reaction is what is the same. We tend to decide whether a court is fair by reading the verdict first.

Here is a test worth running at your own table. Name a court, a referee or a newspaper that you trust. Now ask whether you would still trust it the day it rules against you. If the honest answer is no, then you never trusted the court. You trusted the result.

## Movement is easy to photograph and progress takes longer to prove

Results are not the only thing we mistake for proof. Sometimes mere activity is enough.

It is a Saturday afternoon in February 2025. You work for the federal government. An email arrives from an address you have never seen. It asks you to reply with five bullet points about what you did last week. It suggests that not replying could count as quitting.

That email was real. It went to federal workers across the government, as part of the Department of Government Efficiency effort led by Elon Musk. Over the next months the federal civilian workforce shrank sharply. Hundreds of thousands of federal workers left in 2025.

Nobody could say nothing was happening. Offices emptied. Contracts ended. A website posted large savings numbers.

Musk had talked about cutting two trillion dollars. The effort later claimed about 215 billion, roughly a tenth of that. Congress's Government Accountability Office examined the roughly 110 billion credited to canceled contracts, grants and leases, and it found errors and claims it could not verify. A smaller workforce is easy to count. Whether the government now works better, and whether the promised savings survive once you count the costs, is much harder to measure. A person who has watched waste survive five reform efforts in a row might welcome someone who finally shakes the building. A person who depends on a veterans office or a weather service might ask what went out the door with the boxes. Both of them deserve more than a photo of an empty desk.

Movement is easy to photograph. Progress takes longer to prove.

World football shows a different danger, one that no payroll number can measure, and it happened fast.

Say you run a small national football association. You have forty clubs, one training center and a budget that makes every trip to a tournament a hard decision. On a Tuesday in late July 2026 you read that FIFA, which runs world soccer and owns the World Cup, has a plan. It wants to put the commercial rights to its biggest tournaments, the World Cup included, into a new company worth about twenty billion dollars, and sell a minority stake of about a fifth to private investors. You were not asked. You have until September 19 to say yes, and there is a payment on the table if you do. How do you feel?

FIFA President Gianni Infantino said the money would help grow the game in places that need it most. That may even have been true. But Europe's football associations threatened to boycott FIFA competitions altogether. The confederations for North America and Asia came out against it too. One of Infantino's own senior advisers resigned in protest. His chief operating officer told reporters the plan was the project of one person.

By Friday it was dead. The plan lived four days, and some milk lasts longer.

The fight was never really between people who wanted football to grow and people who did not. It was about who gets to propose a change that big, who has to be asked first, and whether a big promised payoff is a good enough reason to skip the asking. You can think the money idea deserved a hearing and still think the process was wrong. Once a stake in the commercial future of the World Cup has been sold, it is very hard to ask for it back.

These cases are not equal. A federal agency is not a sports league. The government story warns us not to mistake activity for progress. The football story adds a second warning. Some decisions are not dangerous because they are fast. They are dangerous because they are fast, shared, and hard to take back. The World Cup is a shared inheritance for everyone who has ever kicked a ball, and a fifth of the company selling its rights nearly changed hands in four days without most of football being asked.

## Everyone loves Robin Hood because of where the story puts you

Most of us met Robin Hood as children. In the cartoon many of us grew up with he was a fox, which is an odd animal to pick for a man you are supposed to trust. He robbed the rich and gave to the poor, and we cheered every arrow.

Now tell it from the other side of the forest. You drive a cart for a wool merchant in Nottingham. The wool is not yours. The wages are barely yours. A man in green steps out of the trees and takes the cart, and you go home and explain to the merchant why he should still pay you this week.

Nobody ever told us that version. The story was built so that we would stand with the poor, and there is nothing wrong with standing with the poor. It does mean the storyteller chose our seat before the first arrow flew. By the time we are asked whether robbery is acceptable, we already know which side of the forest we live on.

That is the oldest trick in the book on persuasion. You do not have to defend the method if you can choose who the audience thinks is paying for it.

A leader does not always need the public to avoid the cost. Sometimes the public accepts it because the story gives the cost a target.

On the evening of November 8, 2016, India's prime minister, Narendra Modi, went on national television. At midnight, he said, the country's 500-rupee and 1,000-rupee notes would stop being money. Those two notes made up about 86 percent of all the cash in India. People had about four hours.

Say you are in a queue in Lucknow. You are invented. The queue was not. You sell tea from a cart. Nearly every customer pays in cash, and your savings sit in a steel tin under the bed, mostly in 500s. The next morning you are outside a bank at six. The line goes around the block. It is still there the next day, and the day after that.

The government's story was a Robin Hood story. The target was "black money," the untaxed cash that corrupt rich people were said to hide in mattresses and walls. You were being asked to stand in line so that someone much richer than you would lose far more. A lot of people in those lines said they thought it was worth it.

In 2018 India's central bank finished counting. About 99.3 percent of the banned money had come back into the banks. Most of the mattress money, if it existed, had found its way through the laundry. Supporters point to other gains, like more people filing taxes and more people paying with their phones. Those gains may be real. They were not the promise that got people into the queue.

In 2019 Modi's party won 303 seats in parliament, up from 282.

That is the part worth thinking about. The people who pay for a bold move will often cheer it anyway, if the story tells them someone richer is paying more. The queue is real. The rich man suffering somewhere across town is a picture in your head. The picture can carry you through a lot of mornings.

The Robin Hood story has a darker version, and it has guns in it.

In the early 1980s, on a hillside in Medellín, Colombia, families were living beside a city garbage dump. A man paid to build them hundreds of brick houses. He called the project Medellín Without Slums. Today most people call the neighborhood Barrio Pablo Escobar, and his face is painted on its walls.

Escobar also ran the most violent cocaine business of his era. His war on the Colombian state killed police officers, judges, journalists and a leading candidate for president. Both things are true of the same man. The house and the grave were paid for with the same money.

Say you are eight years old in 1985 and you have just moved from the dump into one of those houses. It has a door that locks. You will not be the one who decides what Pablo was. You will grow up in a place where the answer has already been painted on the wall.

In April 2020, with Mexico locked down by the pandemic, boxes of rice, oil, sugar and toilet paper started turning up at the doors of old people in Guadalajara. The boxes carried the face of Joaquín Guzmán, the drug lord known as El Chapo, who was by then in a maximum-security prison in Colorado. They came from a company run by his daughter. The brand was El Chapo 701, named for his rank on a 2009 Forbes list of the world's richest people. It may be the only charity ever named after a place on a rich list.

A food box is a kind thing to receive when you are hungry. It is also a receipt. In a neighborhood where the cartel paid for the roof or the rice, speaking against the cartel is ungrateful at best. At worst it is dangerous. The gift and the silence arrive in the same box.

You might think that sort of pressure only works where there are guns. It works almost everywhere, and a psychologist named Solomon Asch showed how cheaply.

In 1951, at Swarthmore College, Asch sat students at a table with a group of actors and showed them lines on a card. The job was to say which line matched another in length. The answer was obvious. One by one the actors gave the same wrong answer out loud. About three quarters of the real students went along with the group at least once. Nobody threatened them. Nobody gave them rice. They just did not want to be the only person at the table who said something different.

The lines were not close, and it was a psychology lab, and people still gave in. Now think about a person whose neighbor's face is painted on the wall outside, or whose whole family is in yellow shirts, or whose queue is full of people saying it is worth it. Public agreement is cheap to give and expensive to withdraw. Most of us, most of the time, give it.

## The people who say no are sometimes the problem and sometimes the point

If public agreement is that cheap, then the rules and people built to say no start to look precious. So the easy answer is more checks. More sign-offs, more votes, more chances for someone to say stop.

But anyone who has waited on an institution knows the problem with that. A safeguard and an excuse can look identical from the outside.

The European Union lives with this every day. On many questions its member governments vote by a weighted majority. On some of the most serious ones, like parts of foreign policy and taxes, every single country has to agree. That rule protects small countries from being dragged into things they cannot accept. It also means one government can stop twenty-six others.

For years that one government was often Hungary. Prime Minister Viktor Orbán used his veto again and again to slow down European sanctions on Russia and aid for Ukraine. To people in Kyiv, the right to say no looked a lot like the right to let them wait. Then Hungary changed its mind.

Say you have just won an election. You are Péter Magyar, a former member of Orbán's own party who broke with it and built a new one. In April 2026 your party wins 141 of the 199 seats in parliament. In Hungary, two-thirds of parliament can rewrite the constitution. Two-thirds of 199 is 133. You have eight seats to spare.

Here is the part that should make you pause. Orbán also once had a two-thirds majority. He won one in 2010, and he used it to write a new constitution and to remake the courts and the media rules. Magyar promised the opposite. He said he would bring back checks and balances, fight corruption and win back the European Union money that had been frozen over the rule of law.

The mandate is the same size. The promise is the reverse. What decides which story this becomes?

If the point of strong power is to rebuild institutions that can resist the next strong leader, then speed is the wrong scorecard. A better test is whether the courts and newspapers Magyar leaves behind can one day say no to Magyar. Brazil is running that test right now, in public, with a family barbecue in every living room. A leader can use a big mandate to make power answer to people, or to make it more personal. Election night does not tell you which. Only the next few years will.

There is no neat formula here. Too little room to act can freeze a bad situation in place. Too much room makes a whole country depend on the character of one person. Democracy is the work of keeping both of those dangers in view at once, without giving up the need to act or the right to object.

## Speed means something different where nobody can object

This is where the comparisons have to stop being casual.

A coach, a chief executive, an elected president, a cartel boss and a dictator are not five points on one simple scale of toughness. What changes between them is what the rest of us can do about it. Can people criticize the leader out loud? Can they vote, or organize, or leave, or take the leader to court? Those answers change what the leader's speed means. The tea seller in Lucknow could vote Modi out, and chose not to. The boy in Barrio Pablo Escobar had no ballot for Pablo.

In 2022, people in Shanghai spent weeks locked inside their apartment buildings under China's zero-COVID rules. Some could not get enough food. Then, in November, protests broke out in several cities, which almost never happens there. In early December the rules fell away almost overnight. Reuters reported that hospitals were not ready for the wave of infections that followed. The same central power that could lock a city down could also throw it open in a week. The speed was impressive both times, and people paid for it both times.

Russia is the harder case. In February 2022 Vladimir Putin sent his army into Ukraine. By the end of June 2026, United Nations monitors had verified at least 16,431 civilians killed and 48,613 injured. They said the real numbers were almost certainly higher. Every one of those people was somebody's neighbor.

Nobody would call that getting things done. And that is exactly why it belongs here. It shows what the phrase leaves out. Done to whom? With whose consent? And who is allowed to object afterward?

I should say plainly where my own argument thins. Putting a college coach, a tea seller's savings and a war in the same essay is risky. The coach broke a promise. The war broke a country. I am not saying they are alike. I am saying the habit of mind that lets us shrug at the first can, at a larger scale and where there are no exits, lead people to shrug at far worse.

## Back in the stands

It would be nice to finish with a rule. Something that lets us admire bold leaders without any risk. The history will not give us one.

We need people who can tell when an institution has become better at explaining its delays than at fixing anything. We also need people willing to defend a rule, or a stubborn committee, or a lone objector, when it stands between them and something they badly want. The hard part is that most of us only do the second job when we dislike the person doing the pushing. And the third job is the hardest of all. It is saying the unpopular thing out loud at a table where everyone else has already agreed.

So maybe the first step is to keep the relief and to use it. Relief is information. It tells you a problem was left alone too long. It tells you nothing, by itself, about whether the fix will last, or whether the people who paid for it were ever asked.

Go back to the linebacker. He is forty-seven now. He still remembers the pine box. He also watched Auburn go thirteen and zero, and some part of him, the part that loves football, was impressed. Both of those are true, and he does not have to give up one to keep the other.

That is the real skill. A leader can be right about the problem and wrong about who pays for the solution. An institution can be right to slow a leader down and wrong to leave the problem sitting there. A story can be kind to the poor and still hide who got robbed. Your judgment has to be big enough to hold all of it at the same time.

The next time someone finally moves a thing you thought would never move, go ahead and cheer. Then, while you can still hear yourself think, ask four questions. What actually got better? Who paid for it? Who told me the story, and where did they seat me? And would I defend this way of doing things if the next person used it for something I could not stand?

Then sit back down and keep your ticket stub. You may want it later.
